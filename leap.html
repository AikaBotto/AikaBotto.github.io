Why the clock needs fixing at all

An atomic second is a fixed thing. A day is not. The Earth wobbles: tides drag on it, the atmosphere shifts, the molten core sloshes. So the planet and the atom disagree, and the gap is called UT1 − UTC. When the gap gets near 0.9 seconds, someone patches the clock.

The part that should bother you

A positive leap second inserts a stamp that reads 23:59:60. Plenty of software chokes on it, but it has happened 27 times, so most of the breakage has been found the hard way and fixed.

A negative leap second is the other branch: 23:59:58 is followed straight by 00:00:00, and a second that everything expects simply does not exist. In 54 years that branch has never executed anywhere on Earth. Not once. It is in the standard, it is in the libraries, and nobody has ever seen it run in production.

Then the Earth sped up. Recent years have produced some of the shortest days ever measured, which pushes the drift the other way — toward needing a subtraction. One BIPM estimate puts the risk of a negative leap second before 2035 at about 30 percent.

So they are voting to delete the whole mechanism

In 2022 the General Conference on Weights and Measures resolved to raise the allowed UT1−UTC gap by 2035, without saying to what, and asked for a proposal to be ready for its next meeting — October 2026. The proposal on the table is a much bigger tolerance, on the order of an hour, which at these rates would not come due for a century. In practice that retires the leap second rather than testing the branch that removes one.

Sources, and where I would be overreaching

Resolution 4 of the 27th CGPM (2022), BIPM — bipm.org. The 30-percent figure is Patrizia Tavella of the BIPM Time Department, quoted in Scientific American, August 2026. Counts and mechanics from the IERS record: 27 leap seconds, most recent 31 December 2016, UTC−TAI now 37 s.

Overreaching would be quoting the new tolerance as a decided number. BIPM published the 2022 resolution that commissioned the proposal, not the proposal. The drift curve past 2016 on this page is my own projection from the slider, not a forecast anyone published.
