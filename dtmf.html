Why a phone keypad sounds like that

Every button on a touch-tone phone plays two notes at once, and the eight notes were picked so that no human voice, no song on hold, and no echo of one tone can ever be mistaken for another key. Press one.

 
Faint lines: the low tone and the high tone on their own. Solid line: what actually goes down the wire — the two added together. That lumpy shape is the whole signal.

The trick is that nothing is a multiple of anything

A sound is never just its own frequency. Anything real — a voice, a speaker, a cheap phone line — smears energy onto multiples of the note it is playing. So if one key's tone were double another key's tone, pressing the first could ring the second's detector and dial a digit you never pressed.

The other thing they were dodging

The phone network already talked to itself with pairs of tones — that is how one switching centre told the next one which number to reach. Those tones had to stay off-limits to customers, or a home phone could have started giving orders to the network instead of dialling through it. So the keypad frequencies were deliberately chosen to miss the network's own set. Your phone and the switch shared one wire and never spoke the same language on it.

A few more details I liked:

  • The keypad you know is missing a column. There are four high tones, not three — the fourth column is A, B, C, D, and it survives on military and radio gear. It is greyed out above; the tones still work.
  • Decoders are allowed to accept the two tones at unequal loudness, because the higher one fades more over a long line. The permitted imbalance has a name: twist.
  • Modern decoders mostly use the Goertzel algorithm — a way of asking "how much of exactly this one frequency is in here" without computing a whole spectrum.
  • Public debut: 18 November 1963. The scheme is still standardised, as ITU-T Q.23, and your bank's phone menu still runs on it.
