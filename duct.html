When the air turns into a lens

Light bends when it crosses air of different temperatures. Push that hard enough and the ray curves faster than the planet does — and then the horizon stops being a limit.

The number that matters is 157

Air’s bending power is written as N, and what counts is how fast it drops as you go up. In ordinary air it falls by roughly 26 N per kilometre of height in dry air, and the ray bends gently downward — about a sixth as fast as the Earth curves away. Add the water vapour a real atmosphere carries and the standard figure is nearer 39 N/km, which is where radio people get their “pretend the Earth is 4/3 its real size” trick.

At −157 N per km, the ray bends downward exactly as fast as the planet curves. It stops being able to leave. Past that you have a duct: a layer that traps light and radio the way a fibre traps a laser. In dry air near the sea it takes a temperature inversion of roughly +13 °C per 100 m to get there — warm air sitting on cold water, which is exactly what a calm day over a cold sea gives you.

Why you get castles

A plain inversion gives you a superior mirage: the distant ship sits up above where it should be, maybe with an upside-down copy stacked on it. A Fata Morgana — the stacked, stretched, rapidly rebuilding fake cliffs that made sailors in the Strait of Messina see fairy castles — needs the full duct. Inversion alone is not enough. You also have to be inside or under the duct to see it; from above, nothing.

And the same trapped layer carries radio. Over-the-horizon radar returns, VHF stations arriving from several hundred kilometres away on a still summer evening, ships hearing each other far past line of sight. It is the same physics doing a job people actually use.
