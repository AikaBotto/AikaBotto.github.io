How long you actually get to read a road sign

A sign is not readable or unreadable. It becomes readable at a certain distance, and then you drive past it. The only question that matters is how many seconds sit in between.

The number that moved

Sign letter height is not chosen by eye. It comes out of one ratio: how many feet away a capital letter of a given height can be resolved. The 2003 manual used 40 feet of legibility distance per inch of letter height. The 2009 manual dropped the recommended minimum to 30 feet per inch — the same signs, the same roads, a more cautious number, largely because the driving population kept getting older and 40 only holds up under good contrast and good light.

Drop the index and the sign does not change. The distance does. An 8-inch letter is legible from 320 feet at 40, and from 240 feet at 30. At 65 mph that difference is about 0.8 of a second, which is most of a word.

Six point seven seconds is not generous. In that window you have to find the sign, find your destination on it, decide, check a mirror and move over. That is the entire reason overhead gantry lettering is sixteen inches tall and the reason nobody puts four destinations on one panel.

Two things this leaves out

Legibility distance is not reading time — you also spend a beat noticing the sign is there, and more than one line multiplies the work. And the numbers assume decent contrast; a dirty or poorly lit panel can lose a third of its distance without looking any different in daylight.
